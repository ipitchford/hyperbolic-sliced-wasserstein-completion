# Compact completion and uniform statistical bounds for hyperbolic geodesic sliced Wasserstein distance

**Exact boundary geometry in every dimension, a correction, and a complete radial augmentation**

Author: Anonymous · Date: 4 November 2026 · Version 0.1.0-candidate · Text CC0 1.0 · Code MIT (see `LICENSE.md`)

Status: unrefereed candidate. Prepared with AI assistance and checked internally. No human peer review; no proof is formally (machine) verified.

## In plain English

Data such as trees, hierarchies and networks are often embedded in *hyperbolic space*, a geometry in which room grows exponentially as you move away from the centre. To compare two clouds of such points, machine-learning methods use *sliced Wasserstein distance*: project both clouds onto many lines through a fixed centre, compare them on each line (which is easy), and average. Bonet and co-authors (2023) introduced the version that projects along hyperbolic geodesics.

This paper works out exactly what that distance measures.

- **What it converges to (proved).** In the Klein picture of hyperbolic space (a ball whose straight chords are the geodesics), each projection is just atanh of a dot product. These projections stay bounded on average even at infinity. So the distance extends to distributions on the *closed* ball, which is compact: mass that drifts off to infinity converges to the boundary sphere instead of escaping. The distance has an exact maximum value and detects ordinary (weak) convergence, but it cannot see how heavy the far tails are.
- **It barely sees depth (proved).** Two points far out in the same direction are exponentially close in this distance, however far apart they really are. Exact rates are given, with a change of behaviour at dimension 2p+1.
- **The boundary sphere's geometry, in every dimension (proved).** In two dimensions the squared distance between boundary points is π times their angle. In three dimensions it behaves like ½·angle²·log(1/angle). From four dimensions on it is comparable to the squared angle, with an exact constant.
- **A published claim corrected (proved).** Proposition 19 of Bonet, Drumetz and Courty (JMLR 2025) says that a certain back-projection vanishes at infinity. A simple explicit function shows that it does not.
- **Estimation from data needs no assumptions (proved).** The distance between a sample and its population shrinks at the optimal rate for every distribution, with no moment or support conditions, in expectation and with high probability.
- **A cheap repair (proved).** Adding one comparison of the distributions of distances from the centre gives a complete metric with the usual hyperbolic Wasserstein convergence.

Related Evidence Press release: *Sliced Wasserstein distance at the critical moment: the noninteger case* (doi:10.5281/zenodo.23295188) treats a different, Euclidean question.

## Reproduce

Python 3.10 or later with [uv](https://docs.astral.sh/uv/). The scripts use explicit checks (not `assert`), so they also check under `python -O`, and exit with an error if any check fails.

```sh
uv run --with numpy --with scipy --with matplotlib==3.10.8 python3 -I -B code/verify_and_plot.py
uv run --with mpmath python3 -I -B code/verify_extensions.py --out /tmp/hyp_check
# paper (Pandoc and a TeX distribution with pdflatex)
pandoc manuscript.md --standalone --natbib --to=latex -o manuscript.tex
sed -i.bak 's/\\bibliography{refs.bib}/\\bibliography{refs}/' manuscript.tex
J=hyperbolic_slicing_manuscript; pdflatex -jobname=$J manuscript.tex && bibtex $J && pdflatex -jobname=$J manuscript.tex && pdflatex -jobname=$J manuscript.tex
```

`verify_and_plot.py` rewrites `data/` and `figures/` in place. Numerical agreement is a sanity check, not a proof.
